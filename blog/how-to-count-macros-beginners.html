How to Count Macros: A Beginner's Guide for Lifters

By Jess Pierce, NASM Certified Nutrition Coach · October 20, 2026 · 6 min read

To count macros, set a daily target for protein, carbs and fat, then log what you eat in a tracking app and aim to land within about 5–10 grams of each target most days. For most lifters, a good starting point is 0.8–1 gram of protein and 0.3–0.4 grams of fat per pound of goal body weight, with the rest of your calories coming from carbs.

That's the short version. Below, I'll walk you through each step: what macros are, how to find your numbers, how to track without losing your mind, and the mistakes I see beginners make most.

What are macros?

Macros, short for macronutrients, are the three nutrients that supply all of your calories: protein, carbohydrates and fat.

  • Protein (4 calories per gram). Builds and repairs muscle and keeps you full. Eat it at every meal, especially after training.
  • Carbs (4 calories per gram). Your main fuel for training and for your brain. Eat more around workouts and on active days.
  • Fat (9 calories per gram). Supports hormones, joint health and vitamin absorption. Spread it through the day and go lighter before you train.

Why not just count calories? Calories decide whether the scale goes up or down. Macros decide whether that change comes from fat or muscle. Hitting your macros covers your calories automatically, but a calorie number alone tells you nothing about your macros.

Vitamins and minerals matter too, but they have no calories, so they don't count toward your macros. Cover them by eating a variety of fruits and vegetables.

How to set your macro targets

You need about 15 minutes and a calculator. Treat your first numbers as a draft: track for 2–3 weeks, then adjust based on how your body responds.

  1. Estimate your maintenance calories. Multiply your body weight in pounds by your activity number: 12–13 if you mostly sit, 14 if you work out 2–3 times a week, 15 if you work out 4–5 times a week, and 16–17 if you train hard daily or have a physical job.
  2. Adjust for your goal. To lose fat, multiply by 0.80–0.85. To maintain, keep the number. To build muscle, multiply by 1.05–1.10.
  3. Set protein. Eat 0.8–1.0 g per pound of goal body weight. Multiply the grams by 4 to get calories.
  4. Set fat. Eat 0.3–0.4 g per pound of goal body weight. Multiply the grams by 9 to get calories.
  5. Fill the rest with carbs. Subtract your protein and fat calories from your total, then divide by 4.

Example: a 160 lb lifter who trains 5 days a week and wants to lose fat

Carbs(2,040 − 576 − 504) ÷ 4240 g

Daily targets: 144 g protein, 240 g carbs and 56 g fat. Yes, that's a lot of carbs. That's the point. Carbs fuel your training, and better training protects your muscle while you lose fat.

How to track your macros

You don't need to be perfect. Landing within about 5–10 g of each target most days is a win.

  • Use an app. I recommend MyFitnessPal. Scan barcodes and save the meals you repeat.
  • Weigh, don't eyeball, at first. A $15 food scale is the best tool you'll buy. Weigh in grams for 2–3 weeks to train your eye.
  • Let the app do the math. Weigh the food, enter that weight, and the app works out the protein, carbs and fat.
  • Log before you eat. Planning the day in the morning beats guessing at night.
  • Keep meals simple. Chicken, rice and roasted veggies take 30 seconds to log. A 12-ingredient casserole does not.
  • Repeat breakfasts and lunches. Fewer decisions, easier logging and more room for flexible dinners.
  • Count the sneaky stuff. Cooking oil, sauces, creamer and bites off your kid's plate add up fast.
  • Check trends, not single days. Look at your weekly average weight and how training feels before you change anything.

An easy way to build each plate

Start with protein: 1–2 palm-sized portions, or about 25–50 g. Add 1–2 cupped handfuls of carbs, and go bigger around workouts. Fill the rest of the plate with veggies or fruit. Finish with 1–2 thumb-sized portions of fat, counting oil and sauces.

Common macro-counting mistakes

  • Confusing food weight with protein grams. This is the most common beginner mistake. A 150 g serving of Greek yogurt has about 15 g of protein, not 150. Log the food's weight and let the app calculate the macros.
  • Cutting carbs to lose faster. Your training suffers, you lose muscle, and the weight comes back.
  • Under-eating protein. It's the macro most people miss, and it's the one that protects your muscle.
  • Changing numbers after three days. Give any target 2–3 weeks. If your weekly average weight hasn't moved by then, adjust calories by 5–10%, mostly from carbs or fat.
  • All-or-nothing weekends. One off-plan meal is fine. Logging it anyway keeps you honest and on track.
  • Eating back every workout. Your activity number already accounts for your training.

Do you have to count macros forever?

No. Tracking is a learning tool that teaches portions and balance. Most of my clients track closely at first, then shift to a more intuitive approach once the habits stick.
